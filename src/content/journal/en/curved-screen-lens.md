Once I had chosen the Moto X, I still needed one piece: something to give its flat display the curved surface I was looking for. In [The feel of a picture tube](/journal/repurposed-display/), I described why that physical presence mattered to me. Here, the question is how to fit it: which lens should sit in front of the phone, and how much room would it need inside the deck?

I was looking for a piece roughly 6 cm high, close to a 4:3 shape. It needed to look like a small television screen while leaving the rest of the phone usable: another part of the same display is intended for the frequency scale.

## Finding a piece to reuse

I could not find an affordable new part that met the need. Having one made to order seemed disproportionate, and salvaging the front of a real picture tube would have involved more difficult dismantling than I wanted to take on for this search.

Small slide viewers offered another route. In the models I was looking at, a battery powered a lamp, a slide slipped into a slot, and a lens magnified the illuminated image. This little object for examining a photograph contained the curved piece I was after.

I found them second-hand for less than €10. The harder part was finding the right proportions: many had lenses about 4 cm high, too small for the project. I began buying viewers to take apart and try their optics in front of the screen.

## The OPO: three lenses and a setback problem

The first viewer that gave me a promising option was an OPO. Inside, I found three stacked magnifying lenses, with small gaps that I estimate at around half a centimetre. The assembly gave useful magnification, but it meant moving the phone further back.

In my trial, it needed almost 2 cm between the front of the optics and the display behind them. That setback complicated the placement of the frequency scale, since it needs to use another area of the same screen. A suitable position for the video picture became a constraint for the rest of the front panel.

The shape was not quite right either. The lens was a little too narrow for 4:3, and its height was closer to 4.5–5 cm. It was the nearest match I had found at that point, but I was not convinced enough to stop looking.

## The Panavue 9: a single lens

I then found a Panavue 9, again bought for less than €10. Its lens measures about 70 mm wide by 53 mm high, very close to 4:3. At that width, an exact 4:3 rectangle would be 52.5 mm high. It was still short of the 6 cm I had first envisaged, but its proportions were a much better match for the picture I wanted to show.

Taking it apart brought a welcome surprise: a single, thicker lens replaced the OPO’s stack. To my eyes, the effect was almost the same. I could see no scratches on the unit I bought, and it gave me the curved-screen look I was after.

The fit looked more promising. I adjusted the size of the picture on the phone to match the lens while keeping the video’s aspect ratio. The near-4:3 shape let me avoid the cropping a narrower opening would have required.

## The option I am leaning towards

The Panavue 9 lens is now the one I am leaning towards. It combines a more suitable shape, a single optical element and the appearance I wanted. That preference comes from these trials; it does not yet describe a final mounting inside the chassis.

Comparing it with the OPO helped me define the need more precisely. Curvature alone is not enough: size, proportions and distance from the display all matter together, especially when the phone must also carry the frequency scale. The part salvaged from a slide viewer is useful because it addresses those constraints while giving the image the period appearance I wanted to recover.
